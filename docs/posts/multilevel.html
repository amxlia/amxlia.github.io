Multilevel Modelling of Life Satisfaction

This analysis is purely for fun and is not associated with any of my work I would usually properly weight this and consider the survey design but not today.

ImportantRQ

Are people happier when they feel free?

  • does feeling personally free = higher life satisfaction?
  • and does living in a free country strengthen or weaken that relationship?

Data: individual level data from the World values survey 2017-2022 and country level data from the varieties of democracy institute V-Dem dataset

Haerpfer, C., Inglehart, R., Moreno, A., Welzel, C., Kizilova, K., Diez-Medrano, J., Lagos, M., Norris, P., Ponarin, E. & Puranen B. (2022): World Values Survey Wave 7 (2017-2022) Cross-National Data-Set. Version: 4.0.0. World Values Survey Association. DOI: doi.org/10.14281/18241.18

Variables

Role Variable Source Notes
Outcome Life satisfaction (Q49) WVS 0–10 scale
Focal L1 Perceived freedom (Q48) WVS Group-mean centred
L1 covariates Age (Q262 / X003R), sex (Q260), marital status (Q273), social class (Q287), income (Q288R), education (Q275), employment (employment) WVS Grand-mean centred
Focal L2 Liberal democracy index (v2x_libdem), civil liberties index (v2x_civlib) V-Dem Grand-mean centred

Variable transformation

First perceived freedom (Q48) is group-mean centred to separate:

  • within-country effects (i.e., individual deviation from country means)
  • between-country effects (i.e., averaged perceived freedom)

Its country-level mean is retained as a separate L2 predictor. All other L1 covariates and L2 predictors are grand-mean centred (CGM).

Show/hide the code 
# mean centre 
df <- df |> 
    group_by(country_name) |> 
    mutate(
        # focal L1 predictor - group mean centred + group mean 
        freedom_cwc = freedom - mean(freedom, na.rm = TRUE), 
        freedom_mean = mean(freedom, na.rm = TRUE),
        # L1 covariates — grand-mean centred
        age_cgm = age - mean(age, na.rm = TRUE),
        income_cgm = income - mean(income, na.rm = TRUE),
        educ_cgm = education - mean(education,  na.rm = TRUE)
    ) |> 
    ungroup() |> 
    mutate(
        # L2 predictors — grand-mean centred
        liberal_democracy_cgm  = liberal_democracy - mean(liberal_democracy, na.rm = TRUE),
        civil_liberties_cgm  = civil_liberties - mean(civil_liberties, na.rm = TRUE)
    )

Model specification

First need to fit models with REML = FALSE to allow valid likelihood ratio tests via anova(). The final model is refit with REML = TRUE for unbiased variance estimates.

Models are built in the following sequence:

  1. Null model - with random intercept to get baseline ICC (proportion of variance in life satisfaction due to country-level differences)
  2. Random intercept model with L1 predictors
  3. Random slope for perceived freedom
  4. L2 predictors
  5. Cross-level interactions

Importantperceived freedom

Perceived freedom was the strongerst predictor (individual) (b = 0.38, p < .001), where a one unit increase in within-country perceived freedom is associated with a 0.38-point increase in life satisfaction.

Other findings:

  • Income showed a similarly sized positive effect (b = 0.37, p < .001).
  • Marital status and social class were also notable for some categories (people who are seperated or divorced / lower and working class) and showed lower life satisfaction relative to their reference categories
  • Age was not a significant predictor
  • Unemployment was most strongly associated with lower satisfaction compared to employment, and retirement and student status were associated with higher satisfaction

Country level random intercept SD = 0.77 indicates variation in baseline satisfaction across countries.

So now we add a random slope for perceived freedom to see whether the within-country effect of perceived freedom on life satisfaction varies across countries.

We then compare to model one using likelihood ratio tests.

Tiprandom slope for perceived freedom is warranted

adding the random slope allowed within-country effect of freedom on life satisfaction to vary. the likelihood ratio test confirmed this had a significant improvement over m1 (∆χ2(2) = 1391.2, p < .001) and a reduction in AIC/BIC.

The random slope SD is 0.11 and indicates that while there is a positive average within-country effect of perceived freedom (b = .039), the effect also differs meaningfully across countries.

Neither liberal democracy (b = 0.65, p = .34) nor civil liberties (b = -0.57, p = .41) were significant, suggesting that the structural level national freedom doesnt actually directly predict life satisfaction when you take into account individual level perceptions.

The interaction was small but significant (b = 0.12, 95% CI [0.01, 0.23], p = .037): the positive effect of perceived freedom on life satisfaction is slightly stronger in more democratic countries.

However, the effect size is modest and the LRT is only marginally significant.

Direct effects of liberal democracy and civil liberties remain non-significant.

All other fixed effects are pretty stable across the models.

Random effects were unchanged from M4 (intercept sd = 0.58, slope sd = .11) so the interaction might not explain additional between-country variation

Fixed effects are consistent with the ML solution.

Perceived freedom was the strongest individual-level predictor and the between-country effect of freedom was also strong. The cross-level interaction was retained and significant. The within country effect of perceived freedom on life satisfaction is amplified in more liberal and democratic countries. Income and lower social class were the strongest socioeconomic predictors. Age was non significant throughout.

In terms of random effects, there were between country differences in baseline satisfaction after accounting for all predictors (intercept variance sd = 0.6). the random slope sd = 0.11 confirms that the freedom-satisfaction relationship varies across countries and the small positive intercept-slope correlation (r = 0.15) suggests countries with higher baseline satisfaction show stronger freedom effects.
